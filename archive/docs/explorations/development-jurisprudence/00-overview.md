# Development Jurisprudence: An Explainer Set

Status: exploratory and informative, not binding. Nothing here is a rule.
These documents are vectors, not instructions: they point at directions worth
considering and questions worth holding open. Where this set and any project
mechanism disagree, the mechanism wins and this set should be enjoyed anyway.

Inclusion criterion: does it give the reader something to chew on — not
whether it applies directly to this project. Project examples appear as
illustrations, never as requirements.

## The Question This Set Circles

Software development under delegation — to teammates, to development agents —
is a stream of disputes, most of them silent. Every change forces two or more
locally defensible conventions into contact, and one must yield. Someone or
something settles each one. The question is whether the settling happens
judiciously and procedurally, or by default of whoever holds the keyboard.

This turns out to be an old question wearing new clothes. Legal systems have
spent centuries on it: how to pre-settle disputes (legislation), how to
adjudicate novel ones (courts), how to record and reuse settlements
(precedent), how to change the rules for changing rules (constitutions), and
who ultimately decides that any of it is valid (the rule of recognition).
Development jurisprudence borrows that machinery deliberately — and notices
one place where software, especially agent-driven software, is stranger than
law.

## The Documents

1. [Disputes and Jurisdiction](01-disputes-and-jurisdiction.md) — what a
   dispute is, where disputes originate, why most are silent, and what
   jurisdiction means when nobody is arguing out loud.
2. [Institutions of Settlement](02-institutions-of-settlement.md) — the
   design space: pre-settlement, watchdogs, committees, judges; rules versus
   standards; primary versus secondary rules.
3. [Principles, Commitments, Precedent](03-principles-commitments-precedent.md)
   — the conceptual ladder from ontology to mechanism, and the difference
   between a principle and a commitment.
4. [An Ontology of Change](04-an-ontology-of-change.md) — classifying changes
   so they route to the right court.
5. [Precedent and Evolution](05-precedent-and-evolution.md) — how judgments
   become rules, whether precedents evolve fitness functions, and the
   failure modes of accumulated law.
6. [Self-Reference and Its Limits](06-self-reference-and-its-limits.md) —
   whether a system that documents itself with provenance makes dispute
   resolution self-evident. (No. The interesting part is why not.)
7. [Operationalization Vectors](07-operationalization-vectors.md) — phased,
   optional directions for putting any of this to work, plus further
   reading.

Read in any order. Each document stands alone; together they form an
argument, but the argument is not load-bearing.
