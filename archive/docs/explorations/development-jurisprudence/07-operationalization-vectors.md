# Operationalization Vectors

Status: exploratory, not binding. Everything below is a direction, not an
instruction — a vector whose magnitude you choose, including zero.

## Phase One: Name What Already Happens

The cheapest moves merely make existing practice legible.

- Keep the commitment log (ADRs) honest: dated, superseded-never-edited,
  negatives included. The log is the precedent corpus, and under agent
  development the precedent corpus is executable.
- Give every mechanical check a citation: one comment linking the check to
  the commitment or principle it enforces. This is the single highest-value
  habit in the set — it makes every rule re-litigable and prevents zombie
  law at the cost of one line each.
- Adopt the escalation trigger: the "defensible but arbitrary" sensation
  is a novelty detector; when it fires, the change routes up one court. No
  process needed — just the recognition that the feeling is a signal, not
  a mood.
- Let plans state which principles they touch. A single sentence per plan;
  exceptions become visible at the moment they are proposed rather than
  five milestones later.

## Phase Two: Route When Friction Appears

Worth adopting only when the phase-one habits start generating repeated
questions.

- A change-classification line in the plan template: the proposal declares
  its class (canon shape, canon content, derived, behavior, vocabulary,
  process) and thereby its court. Misclassification becomes a checkable
  error — a better failure mode than silence.
- A recurrence review at natural boundaries: scan recent briefs and
  commitments for the same dispute shape appearing twice; twice-settled
  disputes are codification candidates (a new check) or delegation
  candidates (a standing default). This is the common-law loop run
  deliberately instead of accidentally.
- A registry view of enforcement: a generated list of every active check
  and its citation. Cheap if checks already carry citations; the registry
  is just a projection, and reading it occasionally is how rule accretion
  gets noticed before it calcifies.
- Sunset annotations on escalations: when a change class gets promoted to
  a more expensive court after an incident, record a date to reconsider
  the promotion. Courts should be as easy to demote as to create.

## Phase Three: Speculative Instruments

Directions that may never earn their cost; chewing encouraged, adoption
optional.

- The agent as court of first instance: an agent adjudicates routine
  disputes against the principle and precedent corpus, writing reasoned
  settlements; the human becomes an appellate court reviewing only
  contested or novel outcomes. The rule of recognition stays human; the
  volume moves down. The interesting design question is the appeal
  trigger — self-assessed confidence is the obvious candidate and the
  obviously gameable one.
- Dispute mining: silent disputes leave traces — hedged language in briefs,
  reverted choices, repeated review comments on the same theme. Mining
  those traces for unassigned jurisdiction is drift detection at the
  governance layer.
- A codification agent: given the commitment corpus, propose the fitness
  functions that recurrent settlements imply, with citations attached.
  The human ratifies or declines; the loop's slowest step gets a draft
  stage.
- Cross-project jurisprudence: principles and institutional patterns that
  survive one project become a portable constitution-seed for the next.
  Whether governance transplants or must be re-grown locally is a
  genuinely open question — legal history suggests both answers, at
  different times, expensively.

## Further Reading

Each entry earns its place by being chewable, not by being about software.

- Michael Nygard, "Documenting Architecture Decisions" (2011) — the ADR in
  its original, unceremonius form.
- Neal Ford, Rebecca Parsons, Patrick Kua, *Building Evolutionary
  Architectures* — fitness functions as governance; the watchdog
  institution, fully elaborated.
- H. L. A. Hart, *The Concept of Law* — primary versus secondary rules and
  the rule of recognition; the deepest available answer to "who judges the
  judges."
- Louis Kaplow, "Rules versus Standards: An Economic Analysis" (1992) —
  when to pre-settle and when to adjudicate, as a cost question.
- Edward Levi, *An Introduction to Legal Reasoning* — precedent as
  reasoning by example; incidentally the best description of how agents
  use context that was ever written decades before agents.
- Elinor Ostrom, *Governing the Commons* — institutional design principles
  from commons that survived centuries; graduated sanctions, cheap local
  conflict resolution, participatory rule change.
- Thomas Schelling, *The Strategy of Conflict* — focal points: how
  coordination settles silently, and why salience is a design material.
- Meir Lehman, "Programs, Life Cycles, and Laws of Software Evolution"
  (1980) — drift as the null hypothesis, stated as empirical law.
- Peter Gärdenfors (ed.), *Belief Revision* — the AGM tradition;
  supersession semantics with the mathematics attached.
- Douglas Hofstadter, *Gödel, Escher, Bach* — for the incompleteness
  flavor of document 06, taken recreationally rather than medicinally.
