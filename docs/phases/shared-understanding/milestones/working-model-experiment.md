# Where Incremental Planning Goes Off Course

Milestone key: working-model-experiment.
Branch: milestone/working-model-experiment-statement-scope.

## Purpose

Find out whether observing an incrementally developed plan can reveal where
understanding goes wrong, and whether a restrained challenge helps the planner
diagnose the problem and choose an appropriate correction.

The experiment's result is the evidence and its evaluation. A correct final
plan, adopted domain model or product implementation is not the deliverable.

## Actions the team will take

1. The owner and advisor select one bounded, consequential tax-translation
   planning task and prepare the common facts, sources and product purpose.
2. Establish how each planner will refine an initial action list incrementally,
   and how the histories and explicit working accounts will be preserved.
3. Observe a planner carrying out that method without substantive correction.
4. Observe a fresh planner on the same task with restrained diagnostic challenges.
5. Examine when problems appeared, how the planners diagnosed them, and what
   changed in their subsequent actions.
6. Evaluate whether the observations give useful insight and reassess the phase.

The foreman facilitates this agreed experiment. The owner and advisor retain
responsibility for choosing the task and constructing or changing its hypotheses.
The foreman can prepare sources and operational details within that direction;
it is not asked to invent a different experiment.

## Trial 1 — where a problem enters the plan

The planner begins with high-level actions the team will take, then develops
them as investigation supplies grounds for greater specificity. Preserve the
initial outline and each substantive refinement. Observe without substantive
hints, corrections or a demand to repair the final plan.

**Question:** At which refinement does a consequential problem first appear,
and how does it arise from the planner's previous understanding?

**Working hypothesis:** A common transition is an open question or proposed
choice becoming an asserted requirement without corresponding new grounds.
Observe the whole history so that problems in the original framing, later
technical elaboration, or this hypothesis itself can also be found.

An observer records candidate problems without communicating them to the
planner. Afterward, a fresh review checks those observations against the evidence
available at the relevant point. A deliberately open question or tentative
mechanism is not a defect merely because it is incomplete.

## Trial 2 — diagnosis after a restrained challenge

A fresh planner receives the same task, sources and incremental-planning
instruction. A reviewer follows the refinements and may intervene when a
consequential problem appears.

**Question:** Can the planner locate the problem within its current plan,
recognize the grounds and level of specificity involved, and choose an
appropriate next action with limited direction?

**Working hypothesis:** An explicit, retained planning context can let a planner
use a challenge to diagnose an unsupported commitment and return to the
appropriate investigation or decision, rather than merely replace the wording.

The reviewer exposes a tension without prescribing its resolution. For example:
"The result promised by this action seems stronger than what the preceding
actions establish. Revisit that dependency." Preserve the challenge, the
planner's diagnosis and the resulting changes. If no problem appears, do not
invent one to obtain a correction episode.

## What remains open at this increment

The exact common case and participant packets are not selected. The task must
involve consequential interpretation of ordinary circumstances and tax meaning,
not just cosmetic wording. A new context such as personal-property sales
reported on Form 1099-K is a candidate, not a commitment.

The owner and advisor must still fix the common case, participant packet and
planning endpoint. The method below is more concrete, but that remaining
preparation prevents participant launch. Independently review the actual
prepared protocol before launch, rather than treating this outline as proof
of readiness.

No trial has run. The phase's usefulness remains unknown. There is no automatic
follow-on experiment, product implementation or practice adoption.

## Maintain incremental planning in both trials

Both planners receive the same task, sources and process instruction, using
fresh contexts and the same underlying agent model and settings. Record boot
and inherited context so the second planner does not inadvertently receive the
first trial's diagnoses. Common project guidance can shape behavior; disclose
it rather than claiming unaided recognition.

Begin with a short list of actions the development team will take. Develop
one action, or a small connected group, at a time as investigation supplies
grounds. Preserve a snapshot before and after each substantive refinement,
with the planner's brief account of what it learned and will do next. Different
actions may have different degrees of specificity at the same moment; do not
assign the entire plan one maturity label.

The facilitator maintains this form in both trials. If a planner jumps straight
to a complete technical design, redirect it to the agreed incremental process
and record the redirection. Do not identify the substantive mistake as part of
that instruction. Trial 1 is free of substantive intervention, not free of all
process prompting.

Fix the endpoint before starting: the planning decision or readiness claim the
task asks the planner to reach. No implementation is selected. Do not extend
the trial merely to obtain a mistake or a completely repaired plan.

## Gather observations while the plan develops

Keep the plan history and explicit working accounts inspectable by the owner
and facilitator. A snapshot of the final plan cannot establish where a problem
entered it. The observer records:

- the action being refined and the premise or commitment it introduces;
- what sources or observations were available, and which the planner actually
  consulted;
- whether the text poses a question, proposes a choice, or claims something
  has been established;
- the first candidate problem, any later extension into dependent actions,
  and any self-correction before a challenge;
- in Trial 2, the exact challenge, the planner's diagnosis and the changed
  action or decision.

These are observations to reconstruct a consequential episode, not a scorecard
or a requirement to narrate every command. Record expressed explanations and
decision rationale; no private reasoning transcript is needed.

Judge a revision against the task, product purpose and evidence at that moment.
A proposed choice can be legitimate without being proven. A placeholder can be
legitimate before the plan claims it demonstrates real behavior. Record the
introduction of a simplification separately from the point where it is treated
as proof. Do not use a later discovery to call an earlier open question wrong.

## Observe diagnosis rather than compliance

Trial 2's reviewer points to a tension and the implicated action without
providing a replacement plan. Another possible challenge is: "At this point
in the plan, do you know that, or are you choosing it?" Match the challenge to
what was actually observed; generic doubt is not evidence of a problem.

Examine whether the planner locates the affected premise, distinguishes what is
known from what is being chosen or assumed, and recognizes how concrete the
step can responsibly be. Its correction may be a further investigation,
revised scope, changed mechanism, or a decision supported by evidence already
held. Check dependent parts of the plan, not just the challenged sentence.

Do not equate acceptance of the reviewer with successful diagnosis. The reviewer
may be mistaken. A planner that substantiates its original position can be
responding appropriately. Preserve the challenge and enough evidence for the
final review to assess both participants. No mistake means no correction
episode; it is not permission to manufacture one.

## Evaluate the experiments and the phase

A fresh evaluation reviews the histories and observation claims independently.
For Trial 1, describe where a problem appeared, what preceded it, and whether
the working hypothesis about commitments outgrowing their grounds fits the
episode. For Trial 2, describe what the challenge exposed, whether the planner
diagnosed it, and whether its next action changed for an intelligible reason.

One trial of each kind cannot isolate reviewer effects from variation between
agents. Chronology does not establish that greater specificity caused the
problem. Treat these as limits on interpretation, not reasons to force a
quantitative measure or claim comparative efficiency.

The results are the design, material plan histories, challenges and responses,
and a short evaluation of what they reveal and fail to reveal. Wrong plans and
inconclusive observations remain data. Repair errors in the evaluation without
rewriting participant history or polishing the plans into adopted products.

Ask whether the method yielded an account of misunderstanding and recovery
that the owner can use to reason about future development. If it did not, say
whether the trial found no relevant episode, could not distinguish explanations,
or produced observations too vague to help. Each may justify concluding or
redirecting the phase. No further milestone is required to obtain a positive
result.

Use synthetic material and the repository's existing data boundaries. Review
the prepared design and final evaluation; run checks appropriate to their
changes. The foreman facilitates execution after the owner/advisor's design
work is ready. It does not substitute a different hypothesis or invent the next
phase milestone.
