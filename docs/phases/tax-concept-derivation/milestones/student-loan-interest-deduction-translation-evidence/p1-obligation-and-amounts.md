# P1 tax boundary — obligation, double benefit, and return eligibility

Four constituents: legal obligation to pay the interest (26 CFR § 1.221-1(b)(1), with (b)(4) on third-party payment); employer educational-assistance amounts (§ 221(e)(1) first sentence); QTP earnings used for the interest (§ 221(e)(1) second sentence); and dependent status (§ 221(c)).

**Evidence level.** Committed artifacts, statute, and regulation only. This record establishes the tax boundary; it selects nothing and authorizes no implementation.

---

## Citation verification

Verified against 26 U.S.C. § 221 as currently in force (LII) and 26 CFR § 1.221-1 (eCFR, T.D. 9125).

**Legal obligation.** 26 CFR § 1.221-1(b)(1) (flush paragraph under (b) *Eligibility*, heading “Taxpayer must have a legal obligation to make interest payments”):

> A taxpayer is entitled to a deduction under section 221 only if the taxpayer has a legal obligation to make interest payments under the terms of the qualified education loan.

26 CFR § 1.221-1(b)(4)(i) (flush paragraph under “Payments of interest by a third party”):

> If a third party who is not legally obligated to make a payment of interest on a qualified education loan makes a payment of interest on behalf of a taxpayer who is legally obligated to make the payment, then the taxpayer is treated as receiving the payment from the third party and, in turn, paying the interest.

The statute’s allowance, 26 U.S.C. § 221(a), is “the interest paid by the taxpayer during the taxable year on any qualified education loan.” The regulation supplies the explicit obligation rule and the third-party-payment rule. Those are two distinct questions: who was obligated, and who paid.

**Dependent status.** 26 U.S.C. § 221(c) (flush subsection, not a paragraph):

> No deduction shall be allowed by this section to an individual for the taxable year if a deduction under section 151 with respect to such individual is allowed to another taxpayer for the taxable year beginning in the calendar year in which such individual’s taxable year begins.

26 CFR § 1.221-1(b)(2)(i) restates the same bar using “a dependent (as defined in section 152) for whom another taxpayer is allowed a deduction under section 151.” Example 2 under (b)(2)(ii) shows the claiming parent also cannot deduct the interest if only the student had the legal obligation.

Three things stay separate: (1) the ordinary filing fact that another taxpayer claimed/listed this individual with the required identifying information; (2) whether the individual satisfies the substantive § 152 rules for that taxpayer; (3) the resulting § 151 / § 221(c) legal consequence. Claiming is a necessary filing act; substantive eligibility determines whether that claim is legally allowed. Neither alone is the whole test.

26 U.S.C. § 151(e) (flush subsection):

> No exemption shall be allowed under this section with respect to any individual unless the TIN of such individual is included on the return claiming the exemption.

A claim containing the required identifying information is therefore **necessary** to an allowed exemption. With no qualifying claim, no exemption is allowed. A filed claim is **not sufficient** when the § 152 requirements fail.

26 U.S.C. § 151(c): an exemption of the exemption amount “for each individual who is a dependent (as defined in section 152) of the taxpayer for the taxable year.” 26 U.S.C. § 152(a): “dependent” means a qualifying child or a qualifying relative. The ordinary ingredients of that definition, used in constituent 4, are in § 152(c) (qualifying child: relationship, principal place of abode for more than one-half the year, age or student status, own-support, joint-return), § 152(d) (qualifying relative: relationship, gross income, support, not a qualifying child of anyone), § 152(b)(2) (joint return of the individual), § 152(c)(4) (two or more who can claim the same qualifying child), and § 152(e) (divorced or separated parents; custodial-parent release).

For 2025, the exemption amount is zero, and that zero does not make § 221(c) empty. 26 U.S.C. § 151(d)(5) (heading “Special rules for taxable years beginning after 2017”; Pub. L. 119–21, § 70103, removed the former through-2025 sunset for taxable years beginning after December 31, 2024):

> (A) Exemption amount. The term “exemption amount” means zero.
> (B) References. For purposes of any other provision of this title, the reduction of the exemption amount to zero under subparagraph (A) shall not be taken into account in determining whether a deduction is allowed or allowable, or whether a taxpayer is entitled to a deduction, under this section.

Section 221(c) is such an “other provision.” A determination that a § 151 deduction is allowed or allowable to another taxpayer remains operative in 2025 even though no exemption dollars appear on that other return.

**Double benefit, § 221(e)(1).** The subsection is two sentences in flush language (not subparagraphs):

> No deduction shall be allowed under this section for any amount for which a deduction is allowable under any other provision of this chapter, or for which an exclusion is allowable under section 127 to the taxpayer by reason of the payment by the taxpayer’s employer of any indebtedness on a qualified education loan of the taxpayer. The deduction otherwise allowable under subsection (a) (prior to the application of subsection (b)) to the taxpayer for any taxable year shall be reduced (but not below zero) by so much of the distributions treated as a qualified higher education expense under section 529(c)(9) with respect to loans of the taxpayer as would be includible in gross income under section 529(c)(3)(A) for such taxable year but for such treatment.

The first sentence is a denial of deduction for identified amounts (other-chapter deductions, or a § 127 exclusion for employer payment of QEL indebtedness). The second sentence is an explicit reduction, not below zero, of the otherwise allowable § 221(a) amount *before* the § 221(b) cap and MAGI phaseout, by the earnings portion of § 529(c)(9) distributions. Both are amount operations. Neither disqualifies the indebtedness, zeros the return as a class, or selects a worksheet.

Cross-cited definitions used below, not as independent constituents: § 127(c)(1)(B) (employer payment of principal or interest on a QEL of the employee, as educational assistance); § 127(a)(2) ($5,250 annual exclusion cap); § 127(c)(7) (no other deduction or credit for an amount excluded under § 127); § 529(c)(9)(A)–(B) (QHEE includes principal or interest on a QEL of the designated beneficiary or a sibling, $10,000 lifetime per individual).

**Already verified in the P1 loan-qualification record, adopted here without re-derivation.** The statute places the reasonable-period condition at § 221(d)(1)(B) and eligible-student at (C); 26 CFR § 1.221-1(e)(3)(i) reorders those two — this file follows the statute’s lettering. 26 CFR § 1.221-1(a)(1) and (h) still recite the pre-2011 effective-date regime; the current statutory text has no 60-month limitation, so § 1.221-2 is the wrong regulation to lean on. This unit uses § 1.221-1(b) as the matching eligibility regulation, not § 1.221-2.

**Official 2025 Instructions for Form 1040, Schedule 1 line 21** (i1040gi, “Student Loan Interest Deduction”). Four conditions: paid interest in 2025 on a qualified student loan; filing status other than married filing separately; MAGI below the stated ceiling; “You, or your spouse if filing jointly, aren’t claimed as a dependent on someone else’s (such as your parent’s) 2025 tax return.” Separate instruction: “Don’t include any amount paid from a distribution of earnings made from a qualified tuition program (QTP) after 2018 to the extent the earnings are treated as tax free because they were used to pay student loan interest.” The instructions do not restate the legal-obligation rule; 26 CFR § 1.221-1(b)(1) remains controlling for constituent 1. IRS Publication 970 is explanatory only and is never controlling.

## Discrepancies

These do not reopen P0; it is the factual baseline for this record. P0’s heading is now SETTLED (commit `f6e6c081`); the earlier “awaiting independent review” wording is gone.

1. **P0 compresses two different (e)(1) sentences into one formula.** P0.1 describes both employer educational assistance and QTP earnings as “§ 221(e)(1) reduces the otherwise allowable deduction, but not below zero.” The “reduced (but not below zero)” words sit only in the *second* sentence of (e)(1), which is the § 529(c)(9) earnings clause. The employer clause is the first sentence: “No deduction shall be allowed under this section for any amount for which … an exclusion is allowable under section 127 ….” Both are amount operations on an otherwise allowable deduction, and neither is a route-block. The statutory wording is not identical. Constituents 2 and 3 record each sentence on its own terms.

2. **26 CFR § 1.221-1(g)(2) is stale relative to current § 221(e)(1).** The regulation’s double-benefit rule names other Chapter 1 deductions and a § 108(f) exclusion. It does not name the § 127 employer-payment clause (added by Pub. L. 116–136, March 27, 2020) or the § 529(c)(9) reduction (added by Pub. L. 116–94, December 20, 2019). Current statute governs those two amounts. This unit does not treat (g)(2) as the employer or QTP authority.

3. **Committed fact titles cite Pub. 970, not the statute or regulation.** In `packages/content/tax/2025/sli-scope.bundle.json`, `tax.us.2025.sli-scope.not-claimed-as-dependent` and `tax.us.2025.sli-scope.legally-obligated-for-interest` titles cite “Pub. 970 p.33.” In `packages/content/tax/2025/f1098e.bundle.json`, `tax.us.2025.f1098e.no-employer-educational-assistance-interest` and `tax.us.2025.f1098e.no-qtp-earnings-used` titles cite “Pub. 970 p.33, ‘No Double Benefit Allowed’.” P0 rows 6, 7, 12, and 13 correctly place them on § 221(e)(1), § 221(c), and 26 CFR § 1.221-1(b)(1). Pub. 970 is explanatory only.

4. **Statute § 221(c) is “is allowed”; 2025 reporting guidance asks whether the taxpayer was “claimed”; the sources do not settle how those interact.** The statute and 26 CFR § 1.221-1(b)(2)(i) bar the deduction if a § 151 deduction “is allowed” to another taxpayer. Section 151(e) makes a claim with the individual’s TIN a necessary filing act: with no qualifying claim, no exemption is allowed. A filed claim is not sufficient if § 152 fails. The 2025 Form 1040 instructions and Topic 456 operate in claimed/listed terms. P0 row 12 treats “my parents claim me” as the ordinary substrate. That is the ordinary filing fact, and it is not the whole legal test. This unit does not invent a resolution of “is allowed” versus “claimed.” That unresolved interaction is a **selection cost** for P1 (constituent 4).

5. **Legal-obligation fact title overclaims the “someone else may deduct” consequence.** The committed title of `tax.us.2025.sli-scope.legally-obligated-for-interest` says a `no` is “a definite $0 for this return (someone else, not this return, may deduct it).” 26 CFR § 1.221-1(b)(2)(ii) Example 2: when only the student is obligated and another taxpayer is allowed a § 151 deduction for the student, *neither* the student nor the parent deducts. A `no` on this return does not establish that another return may deduct.

6. **Employer committed fact is about interest in a statement’s box 1, not the § 127 amount.** The fact-type title of `tax.us.2025.f1098e.no-employer-educational-assistance-interest` asks whether “employer-paid interest under an educational assistance program (post-3/27/2020) is included in this statement’s box-1 figure.” Section 221(e)(1) denies a deduction for any amount for which a § 127 exclusion is allowable by reason of the employer’s payment of *any indebtedness* on a QEL of the taxpayer. Section 127(c)(1)(B) covers principal or interest, and § 127(a)(2) caps the exclusion at $5,250 of educational assistance for the year. Box 1 does not identify the payer or the excluded amount.

7. **QTP committed fact is a per-statement `{yes, no}` about interest, not the statutory earnings amount.** The fact-type title of `tax.us.2025.f1098e.no-qtp-earnings-used` asks whether “no QTP … distribution-earnings amount was used to pay the interest reported on this statement.” Section 221(e)(1) second sentence reduces the otherwise allowable § 221(a) deduction, not below zero, by the portion of § 529(c)(9) distributions *with respect to loans of the taxpayer* that would be includible under § 529(c)(3)(A) but for QHEE treatment. Section 529(c)(9)(A) covers principal or interest; (B) is a $10,000 lifetime limit per individual. The 2025 instructions’ “Don’t include … earnings … used to pay student loan interest” is a worksheet-line-1 direction, not the statutory formula.

8. **Current product disposition for (e)(1) is a block, not an amount reduction.** Verified in `packages/content/tax/2025/rule.sli-worksheet.json`: on the nonempty route, a `no` (or a mixed all-equal failure) on `no-employer-educational-assistance-interest` or `no-qtp-earnings-used` takes `then: { "code": "SLI_UNIVERSAL_COMPONENT_VIOLATION", "op": "block" }`. That withholds line 21. The statute’s operation is to reduce an otherwise allowable deduction by an amount. P0 F4 already records the collapse; this unit restates it because it is the load-bearing mismatch for constituents 2 and 3.

9. **2025 line-21 instruction list omits legal obligation.** The four printed conditions do not include “you are legally obligated.” Topic 456 does. The regulation at § 1.221-1(b)(1) is controlling. P0’s separate “Obligation for the interest” axis is correct; the omission is in the instructions, not in P0.

10. **§ 151(d)(5)(B) keeps “allowed”/“allowable” live at a zero exemption amount.** For taxable years beginning after 2017, § 151(d)(5)(A) sets the exemption amount to zero. Pub. L. 119–21 made that rule apply without the former through-2025 sunset for years beginning after December 31, 2024, so it is the 2025 rule. Section 151(d)(5)(B) then says that zero “shall not be taken into account in determining whether a deduction is allowed or allowable, or whether a taxpayer is entitled to a deduction, under this section,” for purposes of any other provision of the title. P0 does not mention (d)(5)(B). Without it, § 221(c) could be misread as empty in 2025 because no exemption dollars are allowed. The classification remains a real determination.

## Constituent 1 — legal obligation to pay the interest

### 1. Proposition

I am the person the loan papers make responsible for paying the interest on this loan.

That is not “I sent the payment,” and it is not “this return may deduct student-loan interest.” It is an ordinary statement about whose name, as borrower or co-obligor, is on the terms of a particular loan.

**Who was obligated and who paid are two distinct questions.**

- **Who was obligated** requires a legal duty under the terms of that qualified education loan — typically the signed note, a co-signer’s agreement, or a Parent PLUS obligation in the parent’s name. 26 CFR § 1.221-1(b)(1) is the test. Whose name appears on a Form 1098-E does not settle it; the form reports interest the lender received.
- **Who paid** requires a payment event: who actually remitted the interest this year. 26 CFR § 1.221-1(b)(4)(i) then *relocates* that payment, for this deduction only, when the payer is a third party who is *not* legally obligated: the obligated taxpayer is treated as receiving the payment and paying the interest. (b)(4)(ii) Example 1 (employer pays; treated as compensation to the obligated student, who may deduct). Example 2 (parent pays as a gift; the obligated student may deduct if not claimed as a dependent). Payment by a non-obligor does not move the obligation, and does not give the payer the deduction.

A person can be obligated and not have paid, or have paid and not be obligated. Each question needs its own support.

### 2. Authority

26 CFR § 1.221-1(b)(1), quoted in *Citation verification*. Operative words sit in the flush paragraph under that heading, not in a subparagraph.

26 CFR § 1.221-1(b)(4)(i), quoted in *Citation verification*. Operative words sit in the flush paragraph under “Payments of interest by a third party.”

26 U.S.C. § 221(a) supplies the surrounding allowance (“interest paid by the taxpayer … on any qualified education loan”) but does not itself state the legal-obligation test. The regulation is the explicit rule.

(b)(4)(ii) Example 2 also depends on (b)(2): the gift-payment deduction is available to the obligated student only if a § 151 deduction for that student is not allowed to another taxpayer. Obligation and dependent status are separate bars.

### 3. Scope

**Identified indebtedness.** The regulation’s object is “the terms of *the* qualified education loan” ((b)(1)) and “a payment of interest on *a* qualified education loan” ((b)(4)). Obligation attaches to particular indebtedness, not to the filer as a whole and not to a Form 1098-E statement.

A taxpayer may be obligated on some relevant loans and not others. Form 1098-E may aggregate interest across exactly that mix (P0 F2 C7; box-1 fact-type title: “a lender may aggregate several qualified student loans on one statement”). The statutory/regulatory object is still each loan. Statement identity and filer identity are how committed facts happen to be keyed, not the scope the authority gives the proposition.

### 4. Ordinary facts and evidence

Without reaching a tax conclusion, a person could state or produce:

- For **obligation**: the promissory note or Master Promissory Note naming the borrower; a co-signer or endorser agreement; a Parent PLUS application in a parent’s name; servicer correspondence addressing the borrower of record; a refinance note that replaced a prior note (who signed the new one).
- For **payment**: bank or servicer records of who remitted; a cancelled check or electronic transfer; an employer’s statement that it paid the lender; a parent’s gift of a monthly payment. Form 1098-E box 1 does not identify the payer (P0.1).

Whose name is on the loan is useful support for obligation; it is not itself the legal determination (P0 row 13). A co-signer’s ordinary account (“I signed too”) is evidence of a possible duty, not a conclusion that § 1.221-1(b)(1) is met.

### 5. Dependencies and invalidators

**Must also be true** for a deduction on this interest: the indebtedness is a qualified education loan; the amount is interest; it was paid during the taxable year (including a (b)(4) deemed payment); the taxpayer is not barred by § 221(c); filing status is not MFS (§ 221(e)(2)); MAGI and the $2,500 cap have not eliminated the amount. Those are other constituents and other axes. Obligation does not establish them.

**Invalidators of a previously supported obligation answer:** a later-discovered note showing a different obligor; a refinance that substituted a new borrower; a co-signer release; a showing that the person who paid was the only obligor and this filer never was; a correction that the 1098-E aggregated a loan this filer did not sign. A third-party payment, by itself, does **not** invalidate obligation ((b)(4)). Becoming a dependent of another taxpayer does not change who is obligated; it bars *this return* under § 221(c) even if obligation remains (constituent 4).

### 6. Tax consequence

**Name the operation:** on the indebtedness this filer is not obligated to pay, this taxpayer is not entitled to a § 221 deduction for that interest. The regulation is an entitlement condition attached to the loan, not a reduction of an otherwise allowable amount, not a worksheet-selection rule, and not a return-wide eligibility bar of the § 221(c) kind.

Where the filer *is* obligated and a non-obligor paid, (b)(4) treats the payment as the obligated taxpayer’s. The tax consequence of “who paid,” given obligation, is to count that payment as the obligated person’s interest paid — not to move the deduction to the payer.

**Current product operation (not the legal operation):** a single filer-level `no` publishes literal `0` for the whole Schedule 1 line 21 on the nonempty route (legal-zero `choose` in `rule.sli-worksheet.json`). That zeros the return’s deduction, including interest on loans the filer *is* obligated on if any such loans are in the same run. A `yes` lets the worksheet proceed. Absence blocks `DEPENDENCY_ABSENT`. Closed-empty never reads the fact.

### 7. Correction and retraction behavior

The proposition is about a named loan. Changing “I am the obligor on this note” to “I am not,” or the reverse, must affect only the interest on that indebtedness in a later run. Withdrawing the answer must leave that interest unresolved, not treated as obligated and not treated as a supported zero.

The committed fact is one `{yes, no}` for tax year 2025, supersession `free`. A correction replaces the whole-return answer. A retraction leaves the nonempty route with no current value and blocks on absence. Historical assertions remain provenance (ADR-0010 / ADR-0073). There is no per-loan identity to correct one loan without restating the others.

### 8. Reader-facing explanation

A durable output would have to say, in substance: this return may deduct interest only on loans this taxpayer is legally required to pay; a payment by someone else who was not required to pay it still counts as this taxpayer’s payment if this taxpayer was the one required to pay; Form 1098-E does not settle either point. If the product cannot tell the loans apart, it would have to say it cannot support a figure for a mixed-obligation statement, not that the law allows zero on the obligated loans.

Today, a legal-zero `0` is classified `computed_zero` and explained as MAGI phaseout *or* “a legal-zero eligibility component … answered against deduction,” without naming obligation. A missing answer is a blocked line 21 whose listed codes do not include a named obligation refusal.

### 9. Unsupported nearby inference

This proposition does **not** establish:

- that the person who paid may deduct (the payer who is not obligated may not; (b)(4) Example 2);
- that someone else may deduct if this filer is not obligated (they may not, if they also fail obligation or § 221(c); discrepancy 5);
- that the loan is a qualified education loan;
- that every dollar on a Form 1098-E is this filer’s obligated interest;
- that a co-signer and a primary borrower may both deduct the same payment;
- that dependent status is absent.

### 10. Current representation

`tax.us.2025.sli-scope.legally-obligated-for-interest` (`sli-scope.bundle.json`). Identity keys: `tax-year` only, literal `"2025"`. Domain `{yes, no}`. Title (not controlling): “the filer is legally obligated to pay interest on the loan(s) claimed … `yes` asserts the filer is legally obligated and the route proceeds; `no` asserts the filer is not legally obligated — a definite $0 for this return (someone else, not this return, may deduct it), not a block.”

**How artifacts consume it.** On the nonempty route, `rule.sli-worksheet.json` requires one current `ref` via `conditional_dependency_set`. A `no` is one arm of an `any` that publishes `0` for `tax.us.2025.schedule1.line21-sli-deduction`. A `yes` is not itself a tax conclusion in the rule; it merely fails to take the zero branch. Marshalled as an unbound filer-level symbol, not as a collect source. No `input_bindings` entry. Line-1 subtotal does not read it. Package v38 adopts the vocabulary; nothing in production prepares the assertion (P0 F1).

**What a filer obligated on some of a statement’s loans but not others could and could not say today.** They could contribute only one `{yes, no}` for 2025. `yes` would let the worksheet treat *all* current box-1 interest as if this filer were obligated on every aggregated loan. `no` would publish `0` for the whole deduction, including interest on loans they did sign. They cannot attach an answer to one loan inside a statement, or to one statement among several, because the fact has no lender, statement, or debt key. That is the lossy compression P0 row 13 and F6 record. The regulation’s scope is the loan; the committed fact’s scope is the tax year.

On boundedness, without ranking: the proposition is bounded as an ordinary question about a named note. The *current* representation is not bounded to that object. Mixed obligation is unrepresentable today.

## Constituent 2 — employer educational-assistance amounts

### 1. Proposition

My employer paid some of this student loan under an educational-assistance program, and I did not have to include that payment in my pay.

That is an ordinary account of an employer payment and of whether it was treated as nontaxable assistance. It is not “subtract $X from the deduction,” and it is not “this loan is not qualified.”

### 2. Authority

26 U.S.C. § 221(e)(1), **first sentence** (flush language of (e)(1), not a subparagraph), quoted in *Citation verification*. Operative words:

> No deduction shall be allowed under this section for any amount for which … an exclusion is allowable under section 127 to the taxpayer by reason of the payment by the taxpayer’s employer of any indebtedness on a qualified education loan of the taxpayer.

The “reduced (but not below zero)” clause is the *second* sentence of (e)(1) and is constituent 3, not this one (discrepancy 1).

Section 127(c)(1)(B) defines the assistance that can be excluded: “the payment by an employer, whether paid to the employee or to a lender, of principal or interest on any qualified education loan (as defined in section 221(d)(1)) incurred by the employee for education of the employee.” Section 127(a)(2) limits the exclusion to $5,250 of educational assistance for the year. Section 127(c)(7): no other deduction or credit for an amount excluded under § 127.

26 CFR § 1.221-1(g)(2) does not name § 127; current statute governs (discrepancy 2). (b)(4) Example 1 is a different case: an employer payment *not* excluded under § 108(f), treated as compensation, which the obligated student may deduct. A § 127-excludable payment is the case (e)(1) first sentence removes from the deduction.

### 3. Scope

**An amount of employer-paid indebtedness on a qualified education loan of the taxpayer**, for which a § 127 exclusion is allowable.

The object is not the loan’s qualification (the loan stays a QEL), not the return as a whole, and not “whether any employer assistance exists” as a boolean. The statutory unit is “any amount for which an exclusion is allowable under section 127 … by reason of” that employer payment. That amount may be principal or interest (§ 127(c)(1)(B)); only the interest portion could otherwise enter § 221(a). A Form 1098-E statement is not the statutory object; one statement may mix assisted and unassisted loans, and box 1 does not identify the payer.

### 4. Ordinary facts and evidence

What must **resolve to an amount**, separately from what a person could **produce**:

- **Amount the rule must resolve:** the dollars of this year’s otherwise allowable § 221(a) interest for which a § 127 exclusion is allowable because the employer paid that indebtedness. That is a computed tax amount. It is not something the user should be assumed to supply as a final adjustment. It depends on how much the employer paid toward interest (versus principal), whether the payment was under a written § 127 program, and how the $5,250 cap was exhausted by other educational assistance (tuition, books, other loan payments).
- **Ordinary evidence that could support it, without the tax conclusion:** a written educational-assistance plan; employer statements that it paid the lender or reimbursed the employee; paystubs or a W-2 showing assistance; servicer records allocating a payment to interest; the employee’s own account that “work paid $N toward my loan this year.” Categorical facts (there was a program; the employer did or did not pay) are not the amount. Payment events and source records can support an amount; they are not the § 127 allowable figure.

### 5. Dependencies and invalidators

**Must also be true:** the underlying loan is a QEL of the taxpayer (otherwise § 127(c)(1)(B) and § 221(e)(1) do not attach); the taxpayer is otherwise entitled to a § 221 deduction (obligation, not a dependent, not MFS); some interest was paid this year. The $5,250 cap is a fact about *all* § 127 assistance for the year, not only loan payments.

**Invalidators:** the payment was taxable compensation, not a § 127 exclusion ((b)(4) Example 1 then *allows* the deduction); the employer paid principal only, so no § 221 amount is affected; the $5,250 cap was already used on tuition, so no further § 127 exclusion is allowable on the loan payment; a correction of the dollars paid; a showing the loan was not the employee’s QEL.

### 6. Tax consequence

**Name the operation:** deny the § 221 deduction for the identified amount — equivalently, reduce the otherwise allowable deduction by that amount. The indebtedness remains a qualified education loan. The return is not zeroed as a class. A different worksheet is not selected.

The statute does not say “if any employer assistance, block the route.” A remaining positive deduction after removing the excluded amount is still allowable, subject to § 221(b).

**Current product operation:** a per-statement `{yes, no}` whose `no` (or a mixed all-equal failure) raises `SLI_UNIVERSAL_COMPONENT_VIOLATION` and withholds line 21. That is a refusal, not an amount reduction (P0 F4; discrepancy 8). A `yes` is treated as “no such amount exists.” Absence of any current value is `BLOCK_ABSENT`.

### 7. Correction and retraction behavior

Changing the ordinary account of an employer payment — dollars, which loan, whether it was nontaxable assistance — must change the *amount* removed from a later run’s otherwise allowable deduction, and must not silently establish that no assistance existed or that the loan is unqualified. Withdrawing the account must leave the excluded amount unresolved, not read as zero assistance.

The committed fact is one `{yes, no}` per statement (lender + statement + tax-year), supersession `free`. A correction replaces the whole-statement boolean. A `no` blocks the entire deduction, not a dollar adjustment. There is no amount identity to correct. Retraction of the last current value for the fact type blocks on empty collect rows. One statement’s `yes` can cover another statement’s unanswered loans (P0 F2).

### 8. Reader-facing explanation

A durable output would have to say: the loan still counts as a qualified education loan; the deduction is smaller by the interest for which employer educational assistance was excludable under § 127; the remaining interest, if any, still goes through the cap and MAGI phaseout. If the product cannot compute that amount, it would have to say the figure is unsupported, not that the law allows nothing.

## Constituent 3 — QTP earnings used for the interest

### 1. Proposition

I used money from a 529 account to pay this student loan, and part of what came out was earnings that I did not have to report as income because the payment counted as a qualified education expense.

That is an ordinary account of a 529 distribution and of what it paid. It is not “subtract $X from line 21,” and it is not “this loan is not qualified.”

### 2. Authority

26 U.S.C. § 221(e)(1), **second sentence** (flush language of (e)(1), following the employer sentence), quoted in *Citation verification*. Operative words:

> The deduction otherwise allowable under subsection (a) (prior to the application of subsection (b)) … shall be reduced (but not below zero) by so much of the distributions treated as a qualified higher education expense under section 529(c)(9) with respect to loans of the taxpayer as would be includible in gross income under section 529(c)(3)(A) for such taxable year but for such treatment.

Section 529(c)(9)(A): QHEE includes “amounts paid as principal or interest on any qualified education loan (as defined in section 221(d)) of the designated beneficiary or a sibling of the designated beneficiary.” Section 529(c)(9)(B): $10,000 lifetime limit per individual. Section 529(c)(3)(A): a distribution is includible in the manner of § 72 to the extent not otherwise excluded — so the reduction is the *earnings* portion that QHEE treatment kept out of income, not the recovered contribution.

The 2025 Form 1040 instructions (Schedule 1 line 21) tell the filer: “Don’t include any amount paid from a distribution of earnings made from a qualified tuition program (QTP) after 2018 to the extent the earnings are treated as tax free because they were used to pay student loan interest.” That is a worksheet-line-1 direction. The statute reduces the otherwise allowable § 221(a) amount *before* the § 221(b) cap and MAGI phaseout, and covers § 529(c)(9) treatment of principal or interest, not only interest. Instructions are not controlling where they compress the formula (discrepancy 7).

26 CFR § 1.221-1(g)(2) does not name § 529(c)(9); current statute governs (discrepancy 2).

### 3. Scope

**An amount, with respect to loans of the taxpayer:** the earnings portion of § 529(c)(9) distributions that would have been income under § 529(c)(3)(A) but for QHEE treatment.

The object is not the loan’s qualification, not the return as a class, and not a Form 1098-E statement. “With respect to loans of the taxpayer” is the statutory range. A 529 distribution that paid a sibling’s loan is § 529(c)(9) QHEE for the sibling’s $10,000 limit; it reduces *this* taxpayer’s § 221 deduction only if those loans are this taxpayer’s. One statement may aggregate several loans; box 1 does not identify 529 source.

### 4. Ordinary facts and evidence

What must **resolve to an amount**, separately from ordinary evidence:

- **Amount the rule must resolve:** the dollars of § 529(c)(9) earnings, with respect to this taxpayer’s loans, that would be includible under § 529(c)(3)(A) but for QHEE treatment, used to reduce the otherwise allowable § 221(a) deduction, not below zero, *before* applying the $2,500 cap and MAGI phaseout. That is a computed tax amount. Do not assume the user supplies it. It depends on the distribution’s earnings fraction (§ 72), how much of the distribution was treated as QHEE under (c)(9), whether that treatment was for principal or interest, whose loan was paid, and remaining lifetime $10,000 room.
- **Ordinary evidence:** Form 1099-Q; 529 account statements showing earnings versus basis; servicer records that a 529 distribution paid the loan; the taxpayer’s account that “we used the 529 to pay $N of this loan.” Categorical facts (a 529 was or was not used) are not the amount. The instructions’ “don’t include earnings used to pay interest” is closer to an input direction than to the statutory pre-cap reduction, but still is not an ordinary-language substitute for the earnings computation.

### 5. Dependencies and invalidators

**Must also be true:** the loans are qualified education loans of the taxpayer; the distribution is from a QTP; QHEE treatment under § 529(c)(9) actually applied (lifetime limit not already exhausted); some otherwise allowable § 221(a) amount exists to reduce. Reduction is before § 221(b), so a large earnings figure can zero the pre-cap amount without speaking to MAGI.

**Invalidators:** the distribution was all recovered contributions (no earnings to include under § 529(c)(3)(A)); the 529 paid a sibling’s loan that is not this taxpayer’s; the $10,000 lifetime limit was already used; the distribution was taxable because it exceeded QHEE; a correction of the earnings fraction or of which loan was paid.

### 6. Tax consequence

**Name the operation:** reduce the otherwise allowable § 221(a) deduction by that earnings amount, not below zero, prior to the § 221(b) cap and MAGI phaseout. The indebtedness remains a qualified education loan. The return is not zeroed as a class except to the extent the reduction reaches zero. A different worksheet is not selected.

The statute does not say “if any QTP earnings were used, block the route.” A remaining positive deduction after the reduction is still allowable, then capped and phased out.

**Current product operation:** a per-statement `{yes, no}` whose `no` raises `SLI_UNIVERSAL_COMPONENT_VIOLATION` and withholds line 21. That is a refusal, not a not-below-zero reduction (P0 F4; discrepancy 8). A `yes` is treated as “no such amount exists.”

### 7. Correction and retraction behavior

Changing the ordinary 529 account — whose loan, dollars, earnings versus basis — must change the *amount* subtracted from a later run’s pre-cap § 221(a) figure, not below zero, and must not silently establish that no 529 was used or that the loan is unqualified. Withdrawing the account must leave the reduction unresolved, not read as zero earnings.

The committed fact is one `{yes, no}` per statement, supersession `free`. A `no` blocks the entire deduction. There is no amount identity. Retraction of the last current value blocks on empty collect rows. One statement’s `yes` can cover another (P0 F2).

### 8. Reader-facing explanation

A durable output would have to say: the loan still counts as a qualified education loan; the otherwise allowable interest is smaller by the tax-free 529 earnings that paid this taxpayer’s student loans, not below zero; the cap and MAGI phaseout apply to what remains. If the product cannot compute that amount, it would have to say the figure is unsupported.

Today a `no` is the same undifferentiated `SLI_UNIVERSAL_COMPONENT_VIOLATION` as constituent 2 and the qualification witnesses (P0 F3).

### 9. Unsupported nearby inference

This proposition does **not** establish:

- that the loan is not a qualified education loan (§ 221(d)(2)(A) reduces QHEE at origination by § 529 amounts — a different operation);
- that a 529 payment of principal, by itself, reduces the interest deduction (the statutory reduction is the earnings that (c)(9) kept out of income, with respect to loans of the taxpayer, whether the QHEE treatment was for principal or interest);
- that a sibling’s 529 loan repayment reduces this taxpayer’s deduction;
- the employer § 127 amount (constituent 2);
- who was legally obligated;
- that worksheet line 1 should be typed net of earnings (the instructions say that; the statute reduces after § 221(a) and before (b)).

## Constituent 4 — dependent status

### 1. Proposition

Keep three things separate. None of them is “this return’s student-loan interest is $0.”

**1. The ordinary filing fact.** Another taxpayer claimed or listed me on their federal return this year, with the identifying information that return requires (name and TIN). “Did anyone claim you?” is an ordinary question about filing behavior. It is not a request that the user apply § 152.

**2. The substantive § 152 facts** about my life relative to that taxpayer, which a person can state without applying the Code:

- how I am related to that person (child, sibling, parent, in-law, member of the household, and the rest of the § 152 lists);
- my age, or that I was a student, or that I was permanently and totally disabled;
- where I lived, and for how much of the year, relative to that person’s home;
- who paid my living costs, and whether I paid more than half of my own;
- my own income, where the qualifying-relative path is in play;
- whether I filed a joint return with a spouse (other than a refund-only return);
- if parents are divorced, separated, or living apart: who had custody, and whether a written release (or a qualifying pre-1985 instrument) gave the exemption to the other parent;
- facts about the other person who might take me: their relationship to me, whether more than one person could, and that person’s circumstances where a tie-breaker applies (for example whose home I lived in longer, or who had higher adjusted gross income).

**3. The legal consequence.** Whether a deduction under § 151 with respect to this individual is allowed to that other taxpayer, and therefore whether § 221(c) bars this return.

The real relationship: **claiming is a necessary filing act; substantive eligibility determines whether that claim is legally allowed.** Neither alone is the whole test. A claim with the required identifying information is necessary (§ 151(e)): with no qualifying claim, no exemption is allowed. A filed claim is not sufficient proof of a validly allowed § 151 deduction when the § 152 requirements fail.

### 2. Authority

26 U.S.C. § 221(c) (flush subsection), quoted in *Citation verification*. Operative words: “if a deduction under section 151 with respect to such individual **is allowed** to another taxpayer” for the taxable year beginning in the calendar year in which this individual’s year begins.

26 U.S.C. § 151(e), quoted in *Citation verification*: no exemption is allowed unless the individual’s TIN is included on the return **claiming** the exemption. That is why a qualifying claim is necessary, not optional color.

26 U.S.C. § 151(c) allows an exemption for each individual who is a dependent as defined in § 152. Section 152(a) is qualifying child or qualifying relative. The tests are in § 152(c), (d), (b)(2), (c)(4), and (e), cited in *Citation verification*. 26 CFR § 1.221-1(b)(2)(i) restates the bar using that same dependent-for-whom-§ 151-is-allowed formulation. Example 1: parents not allowed a deduction; student may deduct. Example 2: parent is allowed § 151; student cannot deduct the interest the student paid; the parent also cannot deduct, because the parent was not legally obligated.

The zero exemption amount does not make the determination moot. 26 U.S.C. § 151(d)(5)(A) sets the exemption amount to zero for taxable years beginning after 2017 (the 2025-effective text, after Pub. L. 119–21). Section 151(d)(5)(B), quoted in *Citation verification*: for any other provision of the title, that zero “shall not be taken into account in determining whether a deduction is allowed or allowable, or whether a taxpayer is entitled to a deduction, under this section.” Section 221(c) is such a provision. “Allowed” still has operative content in 2025 (discrepancy 10).

The 2025 Form 1040 instructions operate expressly in claimed terms: “You, or your spouse if filing jointly, aren’t **claimed** as a dependent on someone else’s (such as your parent’s) 2025 tax return.” Topic 456 uses “claimed” and defines it as listing the name on page 1. The statute says “is allowed.” The sources do not settle how those two operations interact when a claim is filed but § 152 fails, or any other mixed case. That is a **selection cost** for P1, not a resolution invented here (discrepancy 4).

### 3. Scope

**The return — this individual, this taxable year.** Section 221(c) bars “an individual for the taxable year.” It does not range over identified indebtedness, a statement, or a payment. When another taxpayer is allowed a § 151 deduction for this person, no § 221 deduction is available on this return at all, whatever the loans look like (P0.1). Filer/tax-year scope matches the authority’s object. The 2025 instructions extend the same bar to a spouse on a joint return; that is still return-scoped (the joint return), not loan-scoped. Return scope does not collapse the three things in the Proposition into a single copied listing.

### 4. Ordinary facts and evidence

**Filing fact:** “someone listed me, with my TIN, on their return” or “no one did.” A person can say that without applying § 152. The existing committed fact can preserve that ordinary answer.

**Substantive facts:** relationship; age or student (or disability) status; residence; support; income where the qualifying-relative path applies; joint-return status; custody or release rules where parents are divorced, separated, or living apart; and the competing taxpayer’s circumstances, including tie-breakers. School enrollment months, a lease, bank records of who paid rent and food, a divorce decree, a signed Form 8332-style release, and the other household’s living arrangement are evidence of those facts.

A filed claim with the required identifying information is necessary to an allowed exemption. It is not, by itself, proof that the § 152 rules are met. Absence of a qualifying claim means no exemption is allowed (§ 151(e)).

### 5. Dependencies and invalidators

**Both the filing act and the substantive rules must be in view.** Section 221(c) asks whether a § 151 deduction **is allowed**. Section 151(e) supplies a necessary condition: the TIN on the return claiming the exemption. Without that qualifying claim, no exemption is allowed, so the § 221(c) bar does not fire from an unclaimed, unlisted individual. Section 152 supplies whether that claim, if made, is legally allowed. A claim filed where § 152 fails is not a validly allowed § 151 deduction.

Section 151(d)(5)(B) is why a zero exemption amount does not make the “allowed”/“allowable” determination empty in 2025.

**Must also be true** for a § 221 deduction once the bar is off: the other § 221 conditions (qualified loan, obligation, amounts, MAGI, not MFS). The dependent-status determination does not establish them. If the bar applies, those other conditions never become reachable on this return.

**Invalidators:** a change in whether another taxpayer claimed/listed this individual with the required identifying information **can** change the result, because with no qualifying claim no exemption is allowed. A change in relationship, residence, age or student status, support, income, joint-return filing, a custodial-parent release, or a § 152(c)(4) tie-breaker **can** change whether a filed claim is legally allowed. A joint-return spouse for whom a § 151 deduction is allowed to another taxpayer trips the bar on the instructions’ joint-return wording. Obligation does not invalidate this bar: Example 2 keeps the student obligated and still denies both returns.

### 6. Tax consequence

**Name the operation:** zero this return’s § 221 deduction. No deduction “shall be allowed by this section to an individual” for the year. It does not disqualify identified indebtedness (the loan may still be a QEL). It does not reduce an otherwise allowable amount by a smaller figure. It does not select a worksheet. It does not move the deduction to the taxpayer who is allowed the § 151 deduction (Example 2).

**Current product operation:** a filer-level `no` on `not-claimed-as-dependent` (meaning the filer **is** claimed) publishes literal `0` for line 21 on the nonempty route. A `yes` (not claimed) lets the worksheet proceed. That matches the statutory operation’s *shape* — a return-level zero — and it can preserve the ordinary filing answer. It does not independently establish the complete legal classification: treating “claimed” as sufficient to zero line 21 skips whether § 152 is met. Absence blocks `DEPENDENCY_ABSENT`. Closed-empty never reads the fact. Tests: `LegalZeroComputation.test_claimed_as_dependent_computes_zero_not_blocked`; Track 6 path (g) `computed_zero`.

### 7. Correction and retraction behavior

A change in listing **can** change the result. If a later run learns that no qualifying claim (TIN on a return claiming the exemption) was made, § 151(e) means no exemption is allowed. If a later run learns that a qualifying claim was made, the filing-act prong is satisfied and the § 152 facts decide whether that claim is legally allowed.

A change in an ordinary § 152 ingredient — moved out, became a student or stopped being one, filed a joint return, a parent signed or withdrew a release — must re-open whether a filed claim is legally allowed, for this individual-year, once, without inventing per-statement copies. Withdrawing support for the filing fact or for the substantive facts must leave the bar unresolved, not treated as a favorable “not a dependent.”

The committed fact is one `{yes, no}` for tax year 2025, supersession `free`. A correction replaces the return-level claiming answer. That grain matches the filing-fact object. It does not carry the independently correctable § 152 ingredients. A retraction leaves the nonempty route with no current value and blocks on absence. Historical assertions remain provenance.

### 8. Reader-facing explanation

A durable output would have to keep the three things distinct: whether another taxpayer claimed this filer with the required identifying information; whether the § 152 rules are met for that taxpayer; and that § 221(c) zeros this return only when a § 151 deduction is allowed. The loans are not thereby unqualified. The other taxpayer does not get the interest deduction unless that person was the obligor. The zero exemption amount does not make “allowed” empty.

If the product has the filing fact but not the substantive facts, it would have to say what it actually holds — not that a listing is the whole legal test, and not that the absence of a listing leaves the exemption possibly allowed.

Today a `no` is `computed_zero` explained as MAGI phaseout *or* “a legal-zero eligibility component,” without naming claiming, § 152, or § 221(c). A missing answer is a blocked line 21 whose listed codes do not name this bar.

### 9. Unsupported nearby inference

The ordinary **filing** fact does **not** establish:

- that the individual satisfies the substantive § 152 rules for that taxpayer;
- that a deduction under § 151 is validly allowed (a filed claim is necessary, not sufficient).

The ordinary **§ 152** facts do **not** establish:

- that a deduction under § 151 is allowed, if no qualifying claim was made (§ 151(e): with no qualifying claim, no exemption is allowed);
- that the loans are not qualified education loans;
- that the taxpayer who is allowed the § 151 deduction may deduct the interest (they may not, unless they are the obligor; Example 2);
- who was legally obligated (constituent 1);
- any amount reduction under § 221(e)(1);
- that the zero exemption amount in § 151(d)(5)(A) makes § 221(c) inapplicable;
- how “is allowed” and “claimed” interact in a mixed case the sources do not decide (discrepancy 4).

### 10. Current representation

`tax.us.2025.sli-scope.not-claimed-as-dependent` (`sli-scope.bundle.json`). Identity keys: `tax-year` only, literal `"2025"`. Domain `{yes, no}`. Title (not controlling): “the filer is not claimed as a dependent by another taxpayer … `yes` asserts the filer is not claimed … `no` asserts the filer IS claimed as a dependent — the law’s own definite $0 for this return, not missing information and not a block.”

**How artifacts consume it.** On the nonempty route, `rule.sli-worksheet.json` requires one current `ref` via `conditional_dependency_set`. A `no` (is claimed) is one arm of the legal-zero `any` that publishes `0`. Marshalled as an unbound filer-level symbol. No `input_bindings`. Line-1 subtotal does not read it. No dedicated producer. Presence is enforced at filer scope, which is the right *object* (P0 F6 rows 9–12). Per-statement or per-loan copies are not invented — C6/C7 for a return-scoped condition.

Asking whether anyone claimed the filer does not. The current witness is the latter question. Filer-year identity matches the *object* of (4) and is a compression for (1). That is not a reason to treat (4) as the cheaper copy of (1). Grouping them would hide both the debt-scope loss on obligation and the three-part dependent-status test under one “legal-zero” label.

### Constituent 1 with 2 or 3 — cannot group

Obligation is an entitlement condition on identified indebtedness. Employer and QTP amounts are dollar operations on an otherwise allowable deduction. Who signed the note is not evidence of a § 127 exclusion or of 529 earnings. A later correction of obligation must not invent an assistance amount, and the reverse.

### Constituent 4 with 2 or 3 — cannot group

Dependent status zeros the return as a class when a § 151 deduction is allowed — a qualifying claim plus § 152 eligibility. The (e)(1) sentences reduce (or deny) identified amounts and leave a possible remainder. Neither the filing fact nor the substantive § 152 facts are evidence of employer assistance or of a 529 distribution. Return-scoped identity must not be copied onto statements to serve (e)(1), and statement identity must not be invented for § 221(c). Return scope does not collapse claiming, § 152, and the legal consequence into a single copied `{yes, no}`.

### Boundedness notes (not a ranking)

On the evidence in this file, without ordering the four:

- **Constituent 4.** Return scope matches the authority’s object and can simplify identity and lifecycle. It does not collapse claiming, § 152, and the § 151 / § 221(c) consequence into a copy problem. Two alternatives exist and are not chosen here: derive that consequence from sufficient ordinary facts (the filing act and the substantive § 152 facts), or consume a separately supported tax determination produced elsewhere. “Did anyone claim you?” is an ordinary filing question and is not a request to apply § 152; the current `not-claimed-as-dependent` fact may preserve that answer without independently establishing the complete classification. Asking whether another taxpayer is “allowed” a § 151 deduction would ask the user for the tax conclusion and would fail P1’s selection standard. How “is allowed” and “claimed” interact in mixed cases is an unsettled **selection cost**. Ranking this constituent as cheaper because it is filer-keyed, or as if claiming had no legal force, would both be wrong.
- **Constituent 1** looks bounded as an ordinary question about a named note and unbounded as a current representation: mixed obligation on one statement cannot be said honestly today.
- **Constituents 2 and 3** look bounded as ordinary payment events and unbounded as current representations: each statutory consequence must resolve an amount, and each committed witness is a boolean that blocks the route.

Those notes are not a selection. P1 has not selected a forcing case. The existing compressed witnesses are not, as a set, canonical ordinary facts; the dependent-status witness can still be an ordinary filing answer.
