# Intake

## Entry 1

Here's the skeleton. Three design choices first, since they shape everything below: the ontology is **dependency-ordered** (no entry uses a term defined earlier than its own section, per D16, with reserved entries as the only forward references); every entry uses **one uniform format** whether it defines a thing or an act, because acts are citizens too (D8/D10) and a split format would smuggle in a second-class status; and the document ends with a **register** that the closure check reads mechanically.

**Entry format** — each entry carries:

- *Name* and *status*: `defined` or `reserved` (reserved entries carry a forum reference — T1, T2 — and a one-line placeholder sense so the constitution can cite them).
- *Kind*: one of `citizen`, `act`, `record`, `relation` — the top-level sort. Acts and records are citizens with extra obligations (actor attribution per D13; immutability), so kind is a refinement, not a partition.
- *Definition*: what it is, two to four sentences, no norms — norms belong to the constitution.
- *Individuation*: what makes two instances the same one (D4). For acts, this is usually the event itself.
- *Lifecycle*: how it comes to exist, how it's superseded or displaced, what survives.
- *Provenance*: what its record must connect to.
- *Relations*: typed references to other entries.
- *Borrowing note*: present only on legal-lexicon terms, stating the denotational reading (D19).

**Section order:**

**0 — Introduction.** Purpose and authority (D15 placement, below the constitution). Reading conventions and the entry format spec. The two set-pieces (drafted below). A note that the ontology defines and never commands.

**1 — Foundations.** Workspace. Citizen. Schema and schema version (consumes canon/declaration doctrine's definitional halves). Actor. Act. Record. Version pinning as a relation.

**2 — Claims, facts, findings.** Claim. Fact type (with kind declaration and identity keys). Fact. Open fact. Finding — asserted and derived as subkinds, with **derived finding carrying the T1 reserved rider**: authority-by-adopted-instrument, framing recorded, legal construction explicitly not attempted. Basis kinds (documentary, attested, elective). Supersession as ordering among findings of one fact.

**3 — Evidence and proposals.** Evidence (first-class, own lifecycle, sensitivity inheritance pointer to §8). Proposal — transient, no pending persistence, captured verbatim in consuming acts. Rejection as a recorded act that resolves nothing. The determinism boundary, stated as a definitional fact about where nondeterministic production ends.

**4 — Authoritative acts.** Assertion (captures presented claim verbatim). Decision-class assertions: election records, method/convention records, allocation records — each with borrowing notes. Stance (the T2 reserved entry: the workspace's position-like object, relation to filed position deliberately open). Adoption (instrument framing per D19). Grant and revocation. Filing — defined here as an act, marked as the paradigm legal-effect boundary.

**5 — Derivation machinery.** Rule artifact (with the determinism declaration from A15 — pure function of declared inputs — since purity is definitional of the kind, even though the *norm* foreclosing violations lives in the constitution). Generator artifacts and generator provenance. Migration artifacts, flagged adoption-class per D14's qualification. Run.

**6 — Process records.** Process record as the genus; derivation records and consultation records as declared kinds (D8). Run record contents (evaluated, published, displaced, blocked, stopped — from P11). The evidence-not-input role separation (A25) stated definitionally: a record's kind is one that no derivation consumes.

**7 — Supersession and lifecycle.** The single mechanism; individuation edges and derivation edges as its two propagation paths (D4). Displacement from current state versus historical erasure (never). Redaction stance (D12, marked deferred). Cascade semantics — the section where D4's open detail gets worked, so expect this section to lag the others.

**8 — Boundaries.** Quarantine as a place (D9). Sensitivity inheritance: records inherit from what they describe. Consultation authority (grants) versus operative context (D7) — defined here, normed in two constitutional articles. The **legal-effect boundary inventory**: filing; consent-type grants as candidate; explicitly enumerated, explicitly short.

**9 — Register.** Every entry listed with status and kind; reserved entries with forum links; the closure check's contract (every constitutional term resolves here; every reserved entry is open work).

**Set-piece one — the decomposition (intro §0), draft:**

> Ordinary language uses "fact" for three things this document must keep apart. There is the *kind* of thing that can be claimed — that a person has wages from an employer. There is the *question itself* as it applies to someone — this taxpayer's 2025 wages from this employer, which exists as a question whether or not anyone has answered it. And there is a *determination* — an answer given, by someone, at some time, on some basis, which may later be corrected. We call these the fact type, the fact, and the finding. A fact may be open: the question exists, no determination stands. This is not an invention; it is how the language of fact-finding already works — questions of fact are posed, findings are made, findings are superseded, and no one thinks reality changed when a finding is corrected. One caution about the word "finding": in legal settings, findings are made by tribunals. Here a finding is the user's own determination, made for their own working record — either directly, or through instruments they have adopted. Nothing adjudicative is implied, and nothing in this document is.

**Set-piece two — the borrowing clause (intro §0), draft:**

> This document borrows terms from tax law — election, position, filing, grant, adoption — because they name the concepts this system is about, and inventing parallel vocabulary would obscure more than it protects. The borrowing is denotational only. A term identifies which real-world concept a workspace record concerns; it never makes the record perform the legal act. A record of an election is not an election. It is the user's recorded choice, which a later filing may carry into legal effect. No workspace state confers, claims, or constitutes legal status. Legal effect enters the system only at declared boundaries, each marked explicitly in this document; the paradigm is filing, where the user — not the system — performs the legal act over a fixed representation. Everything before that boundary is the user's own working record: operative for the workspace, weightless to the law. Where an entry borrows a legal term, its borrowing note states this reading; where the law has no term for what the system needs, the entry says so plainly rather than stretching a legal one to fit.

## Entry 2

**Foundations**

**Workspace.** The workspace is everything the system authoritatively holds about one taxpayer's affairs. Everything else — every screen, cache, index, export, in-flight computation — is a rendering of the workspace, discardable and rebuildable from it without loss. This totality is the point: if some other component held the only copy of something true, the workspace would stop being the record and become one participant in a scattered one, and every guarantee that follows in this document would quietly rest on that other component instead. The workspace is the user's own working record of their situation and their choices. It has no legal standing, and nothing in it does; the law enters only where this document explicitly says so.

**Citizen.** A citizen is anything the workspace holds as a thing in its own right — something with identity, whose loss would lose information. Every citizen names the schema version that defines it, because a thing whose definition lives in code assumptions or component conventions has a private meaning, and private meanings escape the record. Citizens come in four flavors. A *thing* is held state — a fact, a schema, an artifact. An *act* is something an actor did. A *record* is an account of what a process did. A *source* is material the user submitted as evidence. Renderings are none of these: a view that can be regenerated is not a citizen, no matter how load-bearing it feels.

**Schema.** A schema declares what a kind of citizen is — its shape, what makes one instance distinct from another, and how it relates to other kinds. Schemas are themselves citizens: versioned, inspectable data, not conventions. A published schema version never changes, because everything that binds to it relies on the binding meaning the same thing tomorrow; change means a new version. This is as true of the documents governing this system as of the data inside it.

**Actor.** An actor is a party who can perform authoritative acts. Today there is exactly one: the user. But every act records who performed it from the very first version of this system, because retrofitting attribution later would mean migrating history, while carrying it from the start means that supporting a second party — a spouse, a preparer, a delegate — is only ever a question of authorization rules.

**Act.** An act is an authoritative event: an actor did something, and what the workspace holds or treats as current changed. Acts are the only way the workspace changes. That includes computation — when adopted machinery publishes results, those publications are acts of the user, performed through the instruments the user took up, the way a spreadsheet's recalculation is the work of the person who built the sheet. The machinery is never the author of anything; there is always a person answerable for every change, reachable through the record. An act, once recorded, is never edited. Its effects can be displaced by later acts, but the event itself is history, and history here only accumulates. Where an act consumes something shown to the user — a proposed value, a representation to be filed — the act captures what was shown, verbatim, so that what the user actually saw is never a matter of reconstruction.

**Record.** A record is an immutable account of what a process did — what it read, what it produced, what blocked it, why it stopped. Records are authoritative state for one reason: they cannot be rebuilt. Current facts don't remember which paths a past run couldn't take or what a suggestion consulted; destroy the record and that knowledge is gone. But records are evidence, never ingredients — no computation consumes a record to produce a fact, because a record describes work and must never become a second copy of the state the work was about. A record is as sensitive as what it describes: an account of a real person's return discloses the shape of their circumstances, and inherits their protections.

**Pinning.** Pinning is how one citizen fixes exactly which versions of others it depended on. A derived result pins the facts and rules that produced it; a filing pins everything placed before the user at the moment of signature; a process record pins the versions of the governing documents it operated under. Pins only work because versions are immutable — a pin to something that can change pins nothing — and pinning is what lets any result, however old, be traced to precisely what justified it at the time.

## Entry 3

**Claims, facts, findings**

**Claim.** A claim is content that could be taken as true here: a value, a circumstance, a relationship, a characterization of the situation the return describes. A claim on its own has no standing. It can come from anywhere — extracted from a document, suggested by a model, typed by the user, computed as a candidate — and until an act admits it, it is merely something that could be said. The workspace holds no free-floating claims; claims appear only inside the things that carry them: proposals, findings, records.

**Fact type.** A fact type declares a kind of question the workspace can address — that a person has wages from an employer, that a filing status must be chosen, that a property has a basis. It is a schema, and like any schema it is versioned and inspectable. A fact type declares three things beyond shape. It declares its *identity keys*: what distinguishes one question of this kind from another, so that wages from this employer in this year are a different question from wages from that one. Identity keys may reference other workspace citizens — the employer, the tax year — but never a document, because a question about your situation exists regardless of which piece of paper mentions it, and tying its identity to a file would make the question vanish or double when documents come and go. It declares its *nature*: whether the question has one answer the world already determines, or is a choice the law leaves open — because these are answered differently and corrected differently, and the difference must be declared, not discovered. And where its answers can be produced by machinery, it declares the *rule binding* that produces them.

**Fact.** A fact is a particular question as it applies to this workspace: this taxpayer's 2025 filing status, wages from this W-2's employer this year. It is the enduring identity that answers attach to and that supersession runs along — when a later answer replaces an earlier one, what makes them rivals rather than strangers is that they answer the same fact. Facts come into existence in two ways: some arrive with the territory, instantiated when a body of fact types is adopted; others are individuated into existence when the citizens they're keyed on appear — a second W-2 brings its own wage questions with it. A fact is not its answer, and this is the one place this document deliberately departs from everyday usage while agreeing with the older usage of fact-finding: questions of fact exist before findings are made, and a corrected finding never means reality changed.

**Open fact.** A fact with no current finding is open. An open fact is not an error or an absence — it is the workspace representing something known to be unresolved. Coverage is a property of facts, not findings: what remains open, what blocks a derivation, what a form still needs are all questions about which facts lack current findings. This is why facts must exist unvalued; a system that only knew answers could never say what it was missing.

**Finding.** A finding is a determination of a fact: an answer, given by someone, at some time, on some basis. A finding is immutable — correcting a mistake means a new finding, never an edited one, because the record of what was determined, and relied on, and computed from, is itself something the user may need to explain. Findings carry their basis: *documentary*, where evidence corroborates them; *attested*, where the user is the only witness — the mileage log, the cash contribution; *elective*, where the answer is constituted by choice rather than reported. The basis is not a grade of truth; it is a record of standing, and it matters because the world downstream — substantiation, audit posture, what a reviewer asks for — treats these differently.

Findings arrive two ways. An *asserted finding* is made directly: the user, presented with a claim, takes it as true here. A *derived finding* is published by adopted machinery: rules the user took up, applied to findings that already stand, producing results that follow reproducibly from what they name. A derived finding needs no separate signature, and the reason is worth stating plainly: it contains nothing but findings the user already made, transformed by rules the user already adopted, through machinery that adds no meaning of its own. The user is the author of both kinds — one directly, one through instruments — the way the results in a spreadsheet belong to the person who built the sheet and entered its values. What exactly this authorship amounts to is deliberately left as recorded framing rather than settled theory; the law has no name for a determination produced through adopted machinery, and this document does not force one. *(Reserved: the authority construction for derived findings — the instrument framing is recorded; a fuller account is open work.)*

**Supersession.** Findings of the same fact stand in time order, and at most one is current. A later finding displaces an earlier one from current use; it erases nothing. Two conflicting values are only in conflict if they answer the same fact — otherwise they are strangers, and no machinery need adjudicate them. How freely a fact's findings may be superseded is governed by declared rules and varies by the fact's nature: a mistaken wage figure is corrected without ceremony, because the earlier finding was simply wrong about the world; a method choice binds until the rules governing that choice permit a change, because there was nothing to be wrong about — the choice itself made the answer. The mechanism is one; the governing rules differ, and they are declared where all rules are declared. Displacement propagates: when a finding is superseded, findings derived from it remain true accounts of what they were derived from but cease to belong to the current state, and when a citizen that individuates facts is superseded, the facts keyed on it are displaced with it. Nothing in either cascade rewrites history; currency moves, the record stays.

## Entry 4

**Evidence and proposals**

**Evidence.** Evidence is material the user submitted as ground for claims: a W-2, a brokerage statement, a receipt, a prior-year return. It is a citizen of the *source* flavor — first-class, with its own identity, its own lifecycle, its own record of when and how it arrived. Evidence is authoritative in exactly one way: as evidence. That it was submitted, when, and what it contains are matters of record. What it *says about the situation* has no standing at all until findings are made from it, because a document can be wrong, superseded by a corrected copy, or simply not mean what an extractor thought it meant. Evidence supports findings; it never becomes them. Replacing or removing evidence changes the evidentiary standing of the findings it supported — a finding whose corroboration is gone is a finding with a weaker basis — but it rewrites nothing and deletes nothing, because the finding was the user's determination, and the document was only ever its ground.

**Proposal.** A proposal is a candidate finding: a claim the system puts before the user, shaped exactly as it would be asserted. Extraction from evidence produces proposals; suggestion engines produce proposals; a computed candidate awaiting a choice is a proposal. Proposals are transient, and the transience is principled rather than incidental. They are produced by processes that are not deterministic — run the extractor again and it may read the smudged box differently; ask the model again and it may suggest otherwise — so a proposal cannot be treated as a stable thing the system holds, only as a thing the system *said*, at a moment, to a person. The workspace therefore keeps no store of pending proposals: nothing waits in a queue, nothing hardens with age, nothing escalates, and no flow requires resolving a proposal to proceed. An unaddressed proposal simply evaporates, and the fact it addressed remains open, which is the workspace telling the truth about where things stand.

What rescues proposals from their own transience is capture. When the user acts on a proposal, the act records it verbatim — the claim exactly as presented, preserved inside the assertion the way a quotation is preserved in a transcript. This is what makes presentation integrity checkable forever after: the question "was the user shown what they signed?" is answered from the record, not from re-running a process that might answer differently today. A captured proposal is not pending state and nothing reads it as a queue; it is evidence of an act, fossilized in the act's own record. Alongside the claim, the capture references the consultation that produced it, so what the system consulted to make the suggestion is reachable — but the capture is the assertable content, not the surrounding presentation; what must match verbatim is what was signed, not the pixels around it.

**Rejection.** Declining a proposal is itself a recorded act. It resolves nothing — the fact stays open, no finding is made — but it is not nothing: the record that a claim was surfaced and declined is operative information. It tells coverage the difference between *never surfaced* and *considered and declined*; it tells the proposing machinery not to raise the same claim again as if new; and in a domain where declining a suggestion can itself be a meaningful choice, it preserves what the user turned down and when. Without rejection records, the system faces a bad pair of options — re-propose declined claims forever, or keep a private suppression list, which is exactly the load-bearing pending store this section forecloses. The rejection act captures the declined proposal verbatim, for the same reason acceptance does.

**Modification.** The user may edit a proposed value before asserting it. The resulting act captures both the claim as presented and the claim as asserted, because the difference between them is itself informative: a finding the user corrected on the way in has a different standing than one accepted as extracted — its basis is the user's attestation, informed by but departing from the evidence, and the record shows exactly where the departure happened.

**The determinism boundary.** A single line divides the system's processes, and it sits at assertion. Before it lives everything nondeterministic: extraction, suggestion, drafting, ranking, any consultation of a model — processes whose output may differ between runs, which is precisely why their output has no authority and must pass through a person. After it, everything is deterministic: derivation is pure application of declared rules to standing findings, reproducible exactly, which is precisely why its output needs no person per result. The boundary is not a limitation grudgingly accepted; it is the design. Nondeterministic processes *propose*; the user *disposes*; deterministic machinery *elaborates* what was disposed. Each side has the properties its role requires, and neither could do the other's job.

## Entry 5

**Authoritative acts**

**Assertion.** Assertion is the fundamental act: an actor takes a claim as true here, and a finding comes to stand. The assertion event records who asserted, when, on what basis, and — where the claim was proposed — the proposal verbatim, as the previous section requires. Assertion is deliberately the narrow waist of the system: however a claim originated, whatever process shaped it, whatever confidence anyone had in it, there is exactly one doorway from *could be said* to *stands as determined*, and a person is standing in it. Everything else this section describes is a species of assertion or a companion to it.

**Choice acts.** Some assertions answer questions the world has already answered; others answer questions the law leaves open. When the user asserts a finding for an elective-natured fact — a filing status, an accounting method, a depreciation convention, an allocation between activities — the assertion constitutes the answer rather than reporting one. This document borrows the law's names for these: *election records*, *method records*, *convention records*, *allocation records*. The borrowing is denotational, as the introduction says, and here the caution earns its keep: a recorded election is not an election. Nothing legal has happened. The user has recorded which way they intend to go, the workspace treats that intention as operative — derivation applies it, forms reflect it — and the choice acquires legal life only if a filing eventually carries it there. The workspace still enforces the law's rules about when such choices may change, not because the workspace choice binds anyone, but because a tool that lets you build a return on a choice the law won't let you make is setting you up; the enforcement is fidelity, not force.

**Stance.** *(Reserved.)* A stance is the workspace's position-like object: the user's recorded posture on a question where reasonable readings of the law differ — this activity is a trade or business; this worker is a contractor; this expense is ordinary and necessary. A stance is modeled as a finding of an elective-natured fact whose governing rules carry standards of support rather than mere permission — the difference between "you may choose either" and "you may take this view if it clears a bar." What is deliberately unresolved is the relation between the workspace stance and the *position* the law recognizes, which exists only on a filed return: whether the filing act ratifies stances, restates them, or merely reflects them is vocabulary the filing entry leaves open. What is resolved: stances are first-class, they feed derivation like any finding, and their supersession rules are where defensibility standards live.

**Adoption.** Adoption is the act by which the user takes up machinery: a versioned body of schemas, rules, mappings, and bridges, adopted wholesale as the instruments under which derivation runs. Adoption is what makes the spreadsheet analogy structural rather than rhetorical. Every derived finding traces through its run to an adoption act, and through the adoption act to the user — which is how a workspace where computation publishes thousands of findings still has a human answerable for every one. Adoption is bounded rather than blank for two reasons the machinery principles guarantee: everything adopted is legible, so there is nothing in the instruments that could not be read; and everything adopted is versioned, so what was adopted is a specific set of artifacts, not the drifting disposition of a codebase. Adoption has scope — a body of artifacts, for a workspace, for a purpose — and re-adoption under new versions supersedes it the way any act's effects are superseded: currency moves, history stays. Declining to re-adopt is equally recorded; the user who stays on last year's rule set has made a choice the record shows.

**Grant.** A grant is the act by which the user places something in reach of a consultative process: this document, for extraction; these facts, for a suggestion pass; this workspace region, for a drafting aid. A grant is specific — to material, to purpose — and its specificity is what distinguishes a grant from its absence: a standing ambient permission to "improve suggestions" is not a broad grant, it is no grant, because a grant whose scope cannot be stated cannot be shown, revoked, or reasoned about. Grants are the authorization half of a pair the constitution keeps separate: grants govern what may be *consulted*; context governs what is *operative* for a task. Holding data is never license to consult it — the workspace's totality is an architectural property, not an invitation. Revocation is the companion act: it ends future consultation, and what past consultations did is governed by their records, which show what was read, under which grant, to produce what. One boundary case belongs to this family by design: moving personal data across the quarantine — into a support session, a lower environment, an export — is a grant like any other, made by the data's owner, recorded like any act, and no more implied by convenience than any other grant is.

**Filing.** Filing is the largest assertion in the system, and the only one where the law is standing in the doorway too. Before filing, the return is a rendering — a view over current findings, rebuildable, discardable, with no standing of its own, however complete it looks. Filing occurs when the user adopts a specific representation of that return, makes the required declaration, and submits it. The act pins everything: the representation exactly as shown, the findings it comprised, the rule and schema versions behind them, the jurat, the signature, the transmission record. This is the declared boundary the introduction promised — the one place where a workspace act and a legal act coincide. Here the weight deliberately changes: everything upstream was the user's working record, freely revisable, legally weightless; the filed return is a fixed historical citizen that no later workspace change touches. Corrections and amendments are new filings that supersede the old one operationally and erase it never. And here the stance question comes home: the filed return is where positions in the law's sense exist, whatever relation to workspace stances the reserved entry eventually records.

## Entry 6

**Derivation machinery**

**Rule artifact.** A rule artifact declares a unit of derivation logic: the inputs it depends on, the conditions under which it applies, the operation it performs, and the results it may produce. Rule artifacts are citizens — versioned, immutable once published, typed against the schemas they consume and produce. Two properties are definitional of the kind, not aspirations for it. A rule is *legible*: its complete meaning is recoverable from the artifact itself, without reading engine code or probing behavior — a configuration file no human can recover the rule from is sealed behavior in a data costume. And a rule is *pure*: a function of its declared inputs and nothing else — no clock, no randomness, no environment, no external calls — because purity is what makes derived findings reproducible, and reproducibility is what lets them carry authority without per-result signatures. Derivation logic means more than arithmetic: applicability conditions, thresholds, parameters, field mappings, dependency relations, cross-form bridges, and the rules governing whether a value may be derived at all are all rule content. A system whose formulas are declared while its traversal lives in an orchestrator has sealed its most consequential meaning and exposed the rest.

**Generator.** Some artifacts are manufactured rather than hand-declared — a thousand field schemas stamped from a form definition, rule families compiled from a source specification. A generator is itself a rule artifact: legible, versioned, its outputs carrying provenance through generator and source alike. Generation exempts nothing; a generated rule has the same identity, versioning, and legibility obligations as a written one, plus a lineage.

**Migration artifact.** When schemas version, standing findings don't rewrite — successors are derived. A migration artifact declares how instances of an old version map to the new one, and it runs as derivation runs: consuming findings, publishing findings, pinning what it consumed. One power distinguishes it: where a version change alters identity itself — a fact type re-keyed, a concept split — migration may instantiate successor *facts*, not just findings. Ordinary rules never create questions, only answers; migration touches the lattice. For that reason migration artifacts sit closer to adoption than to arithmetic: taking up a migration is taking up a reshaping of the workspace's question-space, and the act that authorizes it is recorded with the gravity that implies.

**Run.** A run is an execution of adopted machinery against workspace state. The runner works by saturation: it discovers what is eligible — every derivation whose declared inputs, conditions, and required findings are satisfied — publishes complete results, and repeats as new findings make new derivations eligible, until nothing more can fire or progress requires an open fact or an unmade choice. Eligibility is read from declared state, never from code order; an unasserted choice is an unsatisfied dependency, not a decision the runner may make. A run may stop anywhere without leaving the workspace wrong: published findings stand, unfinished execution has no authority, and a later run continues from workspace state rather than repairing a private result model. The engine underneath is thin by definition — it parses, schedules, and executes what artifacts declare, and contributes no tax meaning of its own. The test is portability: any conforming runner, given the same artifacts and findings, publishes the same findings.

---

**Process records**

**Process record.** Every process that reads or writes authoritative state leaves a record — one genus, with declared kinds. A record accounts for an execution: what it operated on, under what versions, what it produced, what stopped it. Records are immutable, schema'd citizens like everything else, and they exist because current state cannot testify about past process: nothing in today's findings remembers what yesterday's run couldn't reach or what a suggestion consulted before proposing.

**Derivation record.** The record of a run. It identifies the workspace state, schema versions, rule versions, and engine version evaluated; it reports what became eligible, what executed, what was published, what was displaced from current use, what remained blocked and why, and why execution stopped. Coverage observed by a run belongs to its record — the boundary that run reached; coverage available *now* is always a fresh query against present facts, never a stored copy. A later change never rewrites a record; it occasions a new run and a new record. Failed and interrupted runs record too — a run that published nothing still happened.

**Consultation record.** The record of a nondeterministic process: an extraction pass, a suggestion engine, a drafting aid, a model consulted along the way. It identifies the grant under which it operated, what it read — which sources, findings, and artifacts entered its working context — and what proposals it produced. This is what makes "what did the system read to produce this suggestion?" a question answered from evidence rather than trust, and it is the record a captured proposal points back to. Consultation records inherit the sensitivity of what they describe, which for a real workspace is everything.

**Records and consumption.** One asymmetry, stated in the foundations and scoped here: derivation never consumes records — no rule takes a record as input, because records describe work and must not become a second copy of the state the work concerned. Pre-assertion processes are not so restricted: a proposing process may consult rejection records to avoid re-raising declined claims. The line holds where authority is made — deterministic derivation reads findings and artifacts only — and relaxes where nothing authoritative is at stake.

## Entry 7

**Supersession and lifecycle**

**The single mechanism.** Everything in the workspace that can be displaced is displaced one way: a later act supersedes an earlier one's effects, currency moves, history stays. Findings supersede findings; adoptions supersede adoptions; filings supersede filings; grants end by revocation, which is supersession wearing its legal name. There is no deletion, no editing, no second mechanism for special cases. What varies is not the machinery but the governing rules — how freely a thing may be superseded is declared per kind, which is how a wage correction stays free while a method change waits on the rules that govern method changes. One mechanism, many terms of use.

**Currency.** At any moment the workspace has a current state: the set of findings, adoptions, and grants not displaced by later acts. Currency is a derived property — computable from the record by walking the acts in order — never a stored flag that could disagree with the history it summarizes. Everything else the system shows is a view over current state plus, where asked, the history beneath it.

**The two edges.** Displacement propagates along exactly two kinds of edge, and they are the two ways one citizen can depend on another's standing. *Derivation edges*: a derived finding pins the findings and rules that produced it; when a pinned input is superseded, the derived finding remains a true account of what it was derived from but ceases to belong to current state — it is displaced, visibly, and the runner's next saturation publishes successors against the revised inputs. *Individuation edges*: a fact is keyed on citizens; when a keyed-on citizen is superseded, the facts individuated by it are displaced with it, findings and all. Remove a dependent, and the questions that dependent brought — and every answer given to them — leave current state together, while remaining in history together. There is no third edge. If a dependency between citizens matters to standing, it is declared as one of these two, which is what keeps cascade computable rather than archaeological.

**Cascade semantics.** Displacement is eager and complete along both edges: when an act supersedes something, everything downstream of it along derivation and individuation edges is displaced in the same logical moment — the workspace never shows a current state where a conclusion outlives its premises. Recomputation, by contrast, is lazy and honest: successors appear when derivation next runs, and until it runs the workspace shows displaced findings as displaced and their facts as open, which is the truth about where things stand. This asymmetry is deliberate. Displacement is a matter of *validity* and must be immediate; replacement is a matter of *work* and may wait. A workspace mid-cascade is incomplete, never wrong — the atomicity principle's distinction, inherited here.

**Correction versus succession.** Two shapes of supersession deserve their names. *Correction* answers the same question again: a new finding for the same fact, displacing the old answer — the fact endures, its history lengthens. *Succession* changes the questions: an individuating citizen replaced (the corrected W-2 arrives as new evidence, a new employer-citizen supersedes a mistyped one), or a migration reshapes the lattice — old facts displaced, successor facts instantiated, findings following by derivation. Correction is cheap and constant; succession is rarer and heavier, and the acts that occasion it — evidence replacement, migration adoption — are recorded with correspondingly more context.

**Redaction.** *(Deferred, stance recorded.)* History's permanence binds the system, not the owner: the owner may destroy the workspace whole. Between everything and nothing sits legal deletion — the obligation to remove specific content while the record survives. The stance: redaction removes a finding's *content* while preserving the act's skeleton — that something was asserted, when, by whom, on what basis-kind, remains; what was asserted does not. A redaction is itself a recorded act with recorded grounds. Filed returns sit under statutory retention that deletion obligations do not reach. The detail is future work; the shape is fixed enough to draft against.

## Entry 8

**Boundaries**

**Quarantine.** Quarantine is a place, not a practice. Personal data lives in the user's workspace and the pipelines that serve it, and nowhere else — not fixtures, not logs, not error reports, not prompts, not telemetry, not lower environments. The boundary is architectural where architecture can hold it: components outside the quarantine *cannot reach* personal data, rather than being trusted not to look. A policy that says "don't copy real data into tests" is a procedure; a test environment with no path to real data is a quarantine. Where a crossing must exist, it exists as a grant — made by the data's owner, specific in scope and purpose, recorded like any act — and a grant complements the wall rather than replacing it: where structure can make a crossing impossible, structure does, and no recorded permission substitutes for a missing wall.

**Sensitivity inheritance.** Sensitivity attaches at birth and propagates by description. Evidence is personal from the moment of submission, before any finding is made from it. And anything that *describes* personal material inherits its protections: a derivation record over a real workspace discloses the shape of a person's circumstances; a consultation record names what was read about them; a captured proposal quotes their W-2. The rule is uniform — a record is as sensitive as what it describes — which is why records live inside the quarantine with the workspace they account for, and why "it's just metadata" is never an exit visa.

**Synthetic by default.** What makes the quarantine livable is that nothing outside it needs what's inside it. The system's full surface — every form, rule, bridge, and edge case — is exercisable on synthetic workspaces structurally identical to real ones: same schemas, same rule versions, same pipelines, manufactured cheaply *because* schemas and rules are legible, versioned data. Synthetic means constructed — generated from declared structural constraints or built independently — never a real record lightly disguised; provenance that begins with an identifiable person is not synthetic no matter how much is altered. If a bug reproduces only on real data, that is a defect in the synthetic tooling, and the fix is better synthesis, never a copy. The publishability test states the posture: everything outside the quarantine could be published; everything inside serves only its owner.

**Consultation and context.** Two questions about a process that reads the user's situation, kept separate because they have different masters. What *may be read* is authorization — the grant's territory, the user's to give, specific and revocable, with holding-is-not-license as its floor. What *is operative* for a task is context — assembled per grant, visible, and controllable: the user can inspect what was included, what was excluded, add, remove, or replace, and a summary may stand in for detail during use but may not silently become the record. The system may propose context, apply declared relevance rules, remember approved policies; it may not presume grants, bundle them, condition unrelated function on them, or treat its own relevance judgment as authoritative over the user's. The constitution norms these separately; the ontology's contribution is only that they are two things.

**The legal-effect inventory.** The borrowing clause promised that every point where a workspace act coincides with a legal act would be marked. The inventory: **filing** — the paradigm, where the user's declaration, signature, and transmission perform the legal deed over a fixed representation. **Consent-type grants** — a candidate: a quarantine-crossing grant may have real legal character under privacy law, and the ontology marks it as boundary-adjacent without settling the question. Nothing else. Elections, methods, stances, adoptions, assertions of every kind — all workspace-operative only, all legally weightless until a filing carries them. The inventory's brevity is the finding: one boundary certain, one candidate, and a system designed so the list stays short.
